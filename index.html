Welcome to the National Museum of Požarevac.

You are now standing at the entrance to the main building, the central space in which the archaeological development of the Braničevo region is presented across several millennia. Here, you will follow a long and complex historical continuum, from the earliest prehistoric communities to the period of Roman rule and Late Antiquity.

Your visit begins with the material remains of the Mesolithic, Neolithic, and Eneolithic periods, which document the emergence of the first settled communities, the development of agriculture, the formation of complex cult practices, and the earliest traces of metallurgy. In this part of the exhibition, you will see pottery, tools, figurines, and other characteristic objects of the prehistoric cultures that shaped the earliest life in this region.

The western section of the building is dedicated to Viminacium, once a major urban center of the Roman province of Upper Moesia. Here, objects are presented that illustrate the economic, artistic, and spiritual life of the city: jewelry, weapons, tools, glass and ceramic vessels, architectural fragments, and a wide range of material from the necropolises. These finds testify to the activity of local workshops, to cultural and religious syncretism, and to the high level of craftsmanship reached in the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th centuries.

On the eastern side of the building, you will encounter the section devoted to the painted funerary architecture of Late Antiquity. The preserved frescoes, originating from the tombs of Viminacium, reflect the coexistence of Christian motifs with elements inherited from earlier pagan traditions, and they offer rare insight into the visual culture and beliefs of the period.

This building forms the core of the museum complex and offers a comprehensive overview of the material culture of the Danube region, with particular emphasis on the development of prehistoric societies and the urban life of Viminacium. Mesolithic (9600–6200 BC)

The Mesolithic, also known as the Middle Stone Age, in Serbia lasted roughly from 9600 to 6200 BCE. This period marks the key transition between the Paleolithic and the Neolithic. It was a time of major climatic changes after the end of the last Ice Age, shaping the landscape and biosphere as we know them today. People adapted to the new conditions by developing microlithic tools — small, precise stone implements, and by focusing on hunting small game, fishing, and gathering.

A particularly important Mesolithic site in Serbia is Lepenski Vir, located on the right bank of the Danube, in the heart of the Đerdap (Iron Gates) Gorge. This world-famous site offers a unique insight into the complex life of Mesolithic communities.

The inhabitants of Lepenski Vir were exceptionally skilled fishers, hunters, and gatherers, which made permanent settlement possible in a single place, something rare for the Mesolithic. They built distinctive trapezoidal houses with hard, lime-plastered floors, demonstrating an impressive level of architectural skill for the time.

What truly sets Lepenski Vir apart are its monumental stone sculptures, often depicting stylized human heads with fish-like features. Found within the settlements, these sculptures are interpreted as river deities, spiritual guardians, or totemic figures, indicating a rich spiritual life and a developed religion.

In addition, complex burial rites have been discovered. The most important information about funerary practices and methods of interment comes from graves at Vlasac, Lepenski Vir, and Padina. The most notable type is the seated burial, in which the deceased was interred in a seated posture with crossed legs.

Lepenski Vir represents a bridge between hunter-gatherer cultures and early farming communities, demonstrating that a sedentary way of life and a complex culture could develop even before the advent of agriculture, provided there were abundant natural resources. It is exceptional evidence of the ability of prehistoric people to adapt and to create a sophisticated culture within a unique natural environment.

Early Neolithic (Older Neolithic) in Serbia

The Starčevo Culture (6200–5300 BCE)

The Early Neolithic in Serbia (c. 6200–5300 BCE) marks the period of the so-called “Neolithic Revolution”, the transition from a nomadic, hunter-gatherer way of life to permanent settlements based on agriculture and animal husbandry. This is when the first cereals (wheat, barley) and legumes were cultivated, and domestic animals (sheep, goats, cattle, pigs) were tamed, while hunting and fishing still played an important role.

The Starčevo culture is named after the site of Starčevo near Pančevo, and its distribution encompassed Banat, Srem, the whole of Vojvodina, central Serbia, Kosovo, and northeastern Bosnia. Settlements were mostly located near rivers and springs and consisted of pit houses and semi-subterranean dwellings.

It is characterised by the appearance of pottery and ground (polished) stone tools. Vessels mostly globular or semi-globular in shape were made from poorly refined clay with admixtures of chaff and crushed shell. Decoration included impressing, painting, and the barbotine (applied slip) technique. Tools were made of ground stone, bone, and antler, the most common being axes, knives, awls, and hoes. Especially notable are small obsidian blades, volcanic glass imported from Greece and Hungary, indicating the existence of trade links.

An important part of spiritual life is represented by fired-clay figurines, often steatopygous female forms, interpreted as symbols of fertility. Within the Braničevo District, Starčevo settlements have been discovered at Dubravica, Kostolac, Drmno, and Malo Crniće.

The Early Neolithic forms the foundation of later social development - a life oriented toward food production, permanent settlement, and new forms of social organisation.

Late Neolithic (Younger Neolithic) in Serbia:

The Vinča Culture (5300–4500 BCE)

The Late Neolithic in Serbia, which lasted roughly from 5300 to 4500 BCE, brought the flowering and further development of Neolithic communities. Its main representative is the Vinča culture, named after the famous site of Vinča near Belgrade, which is rightly considered one of the most significant prehistoric cultures in Europe. Arising on Starčevo foundations, it occupied approximately the same territory, but developed into much larger and longer-lasting settlements. Some of them had an urban character, with an organized internal layout, streets, and houses built with wattle-and-daub and clay. They are rectangular in plan, with one or more rooms.

The economy of the Vinča culture continued to rest on farming and animal husbandry, but also saw notable progress in the techniques of working the land. Pottery reached exceptional quality and diversity: vessels of varied forms were made from well-prepared clay, polished, often black-burnished, with refined shapes and rich ornamentation—incised geometric motifs, meanders, spirals, channels, impressed and punctured decoration. Vinča is particularly known for numerous anthropomorphic, zoomorphic, and hybrid clay figurines, which represent the pinnacle of Neolithic artistry. They show specific stylized facial features and details of clothing, and are thought to have had a cult or ritual role.

Tools were made of polished stone, bone, antler, and flint, while stone axes reached perfected forms. In the Late Neolithic appear the first traces of copper metallurgy, foreshadowing the transition to the Eneolithic (Chalcolithic). The bearers of the Vinča culture, in its early phases, were already familiar with copper-smelting technologies.

Newer archaeological research has shown that Vinča settlements often had a complex structure. At several sites, double ditches were discovered surrounding the settlement; they likely had a defensive, but also an organizational function, which indicates an evident tendency to control space, protect the community, and regulate entrances and movement within the settlement. This development fits the broader picture of the Eneolithic, when the need to fortify became one of the dominant features of many cultures in the Balkans.

Belovode (5350–4600 BCE)

The most important Late Neolithic site in the Braničevo region is Belovode, near the village of Veliko Laole in the municipality of Petrovac na Mlavi. It was discovered in 1952. by Professor Nikola Krstić, an amateur archaeologist and associate of the National Museum in Požarevac, who already then called it “the most important prehistoric site in the entire Braničevo area.” In the spring of 1987. the site was visited and preliminarily surveyed by Dragan Jacanović, whose results were published in the monograph The Neolithic of Serbia (Belgrade, 1988).

The site lies 2 km west of Veliko Laole, on a plateau with Tabanovac and Krvije. It occupies a position along the route descending from the Beljanica mountains, between the streams Busur and Mlava, and in the immediate vicinity of the Belovode spring. Situated at an elevation of about 190 m, it allowed control of the valley and access to the main watercourses—the Mlava and the Velika Morava.

According to recent geomagnetic surveys, the settlement covered about 90 hectares, while excavations have confirmed the existence of ditches, which places Belovode among the fortified Late Neolithic settlements, such as Stubline, Drenovac, and Oreškovica–Selišta.

Systematic archaeological research began in 1994, organised by the National Museum of Serbia under the leadership of Duško Šljivar, in cooperation with the National Museum in Požarevac, and later with the Homeland Museum in Petrovac na Mlavi. Life at the site lasted roughly from 5350 to 4600 BCE, confirming its affiliation with the Vinča culture. In recent years, specialists from Cardiff University and University College London have also participated in the investigations.

Belovode’s special significance lies in the discovery here of one of the earliest occurrences of copper metallurgy in Europe. Analyses have shown that the inhabitants knew the processes of obtaining and working copper already during the second half of the 6th millennium BCE. For this reason, Belovode is one of the key sites for studying the beginnings of metallurgy on the European continent and an important witness to technological innovations and social processes in the Late Neolithic.

5. Eneolithic (Copper Age) in Serbia (4500–2200 BCE)

The Eneolithic, also known as the Copper Age, represents the transitional period between the Neolithic and the Bronze Age and in Serbia is dated roughly from 4500 to 2200 BCE.

The key feature of this period is the beginning of metal use, primarily copper, alongside the continued use of stone, bone, and antler. The discovery and working of copper brought significant changes. Although copper is relatively soft for making tools, its use for jewelry, weapons, and cult objects points to new technological possibilities and the development of crafts.

Compared with the Neolithic, Eneolithic settlements show greater dispersion and expansion into hilly terrain and strategic positions, often with elevated, dominant locations and hard-to-reach sites. Some centers remained in the plains, but ditches and palisades appear as fortifications. Settlements are smaller and shorter-lived than the large, long-lasting Neolithic centers; houses are simpler and smaller, and the layout is not always strictly planned. In some places, specialized zones linked to copper metallurgy emerge.

The Eneolithic is marked by a gradual transition to a more complex, stratified society with a pronounced livestock component, the appearance of settlement hierarchies in the form of small villages around larger centers, and clearer social differences visible in the wealth of graves and material culture.

The economy was still based on farming and herding, but trade gained importance, especially in raw copper.

Cultures such as the Baden culture (3700–2800 BCE) and the Kostolac culture (3300–2700 BCE) were dominant in today’s Serbia during this period, bringing distinctive ceramic styles and funerary customs.

The Eneolithic was a dynamic period of innovation and change that laid the groundwork for the full florescence of metallurgy and the emergence of more complex societies in the ensuing Bronze Age.

Bronze Age (2200–1200 BCE)

The Bronze Age in Serbia covers the period from about 2200 to 1200 BCE and represents a significant step forward in the development of metallurgy, especially the refinement of alloying copper with tin to obtain bronze, the first man-made metal produced through more complex technological processes.

Archaeological finds from this period point to the development of metallurgy and crafts. Numerous settlements, often fortified, were built on elevated positions, indicating the need for protection and the existence of more organized communities. Remains of bronze-working workshops have been found, as well as various bronze objects used not only in everyday life but also for ritual purposes.

Social structures became more complex, with clearer hierarchies. This is evident in differences among grave goods: alongside simple inhumations, barrows (tumuli) appear with rich offerings of bronze, gold, and amber, indicating the presence of a social elite.

Bronze Age cultures on the territory of present-day Serbia, such as the Vatin culture (1900–1600 BCE), Žuto Brdo and later the Gava culture (1200–1000 BCE; the beginning overlaps with the Early Iron Age) were part of broader European cultural currents. Contacts with neighboring regions were maintained, as shown by imported objects and similarities in material culture.

At the end of the 13th and the beginning of the 12th century BCE, the entire eastern Mediterranean was shaken by major ethnic movements. Egyptian inscriptions from the reigns of Merneptah (1229 BCE) and Ramesses III (1189 BCE) record the incursions of the “Sea Peoples.” By about 1180 BCE, most of the great centers such as Troy, Mycenae, Knossos, and others were destroyed. This period of upheaval, known as the Dorian migration, also left traces in our region. Hoards of bronze objects buried in the ground just before danger struck testify to this turmoil. Such hoards have been found in Vojvodina, Leskovac, Kasidol, Klenje, and many other sites. They mostly contain jewelry and farming tools; weapons are rare.

Dubovac–Žuto Brdo Culture (1600–1200 BCE)

Around 1600 BCE, within the area previously occupied by the Vatin culture, a new group formed the Dubovac – Žuto Brdo culture, named after the sites of Dubovac in Banat and Žuto Brdo near Golubac. It is characterized by lavishly decorated ceramic vessels in a wide variety of forms. Decoration was done by incising, stamping/impressing, and inlay. Another hallmark of this culture is the production of baked-clay statuettes that represent a supreme deity. The most famous and finest example is the Kličevac idol, which was destroyed during the First World War. In the Braničevo area, statuettes have also been found at Klenovnik, Stari Kostolac, Malo Crniće, Usije, Salakovac, Dubravica, and Drmno. Female figures are schematically modelled, with details of dress and jewelry shown very precisely.

The bearers of the Žuto Brdo culture appear to have led a relatively peaceful life, devoted mainly to agriculture and animal husbandry. This is also suggested by finds of gold and bronze jewelry, whereas weapons are very rare. Cremation was the sole funerary rite: the burned bones were placed in urns, often covered with ceramic lids. Necropolises from this period have been discovered at Žuto Brdo, Klenovnik, Trnjane, and Stari Kostolac. Bronze pins, axes, knives, and jewelry correspond to the common metal types of Central Europe.

A special category of finds associated with the Žuto Brdo culture are massive decorated neck rings (torcs/bracelets). Because of their size and weight, they were probably of cultic character.

It is likely that the Žuto Brdo communities continued living in parts of the Morava region even after the so-called “Dorian migrations,” though with certain changes in material culture. To the very end of the Bronze Age belongs the cremation necropolis at Trnjane, where urn graves of the Gava culture are also found. This period ends with the arrival of new ethnic groups and the adoption of iron, marking the beginning of a new chapter in Balkan prehistory.

Viminacium

The capital of the province of Upper Moesia

Viminacium was established in the middle of the 1st century, near Kostolac. The settlement probably existed even at the beginning of the 1st century, but as a tribal fortress – oppidum.

The name Viminacium likely derives from the pre-Roman population – in suffix acium. Some authors recognize certain characteristics of the Celtic language, while for others the name is Latin, and derives from the word vimen (willow), as a reference to the typical local wattle architecture.

The castrum on the right bank of the Mlava River was founded by the legion VII Claudia, with the help of the legion IV Flavia.

Over time, Viminacium became the largest urban settlement and capital of the province of Upper Moesia, and it was the centre of the imperial governor – legate of the legion VII Claudia and numerous state officials and city administration representatives.

A civilian settlement developed simultaneously with the camp – canabae legionaris.

At the end of the 1st and beginning of the 2nd century, Viminacium was an important military base in Trajan’s wars against the Dacians on the left bank of the Danube.

Inscriptions document that Viminacium acquired the status of a municipality during the time of the Emperor Hadrian (year 117AD) – Municipium Aelium Viminacium. After this event, numerous art studios and artisanal workshops were established in Viminacium.

During the Severan dynasty (from 193 to 235AD) the economic growth of Viminacium reached its peak, which was reflected in the private and public life of its citizens.

Under Gordian III (at the end of 239AD), Viminacium received the status of a colony and the right to mint colonial coinage.

During the middle of the 3rd century, Viminacium and the legion VII Claudia became a significant political factor in the Empire, as some soldier emperors were brought to the throne with the support of the members of this legion.

In the 4th century, Viminacium was an episcopal seat. Famous bishops from this city were Amantinus and Cyriacus.

In 441AD, Viminacium was destroyed and robbed by the Huns. The city continued to live for another two centuries but was far from its previous size and splendour. At the end of the 5th century, its territory was settled by the German tribes.

In the 6th century, during the time of Justinian, Viminacium was restored. At the end of 584, Viminacium was captured by the Avars.

At the beginning of the 7th century, historical sources mention it as an important military base in the Byzantine war with the Avars. The settlement of the Slavs represents the end of the ancient Viminacium. The settlement of Kostolac was named from the Slovenized Latin word castellum – camp, a fortress.

Military crew on the Danube and the construction of limes

The arrival of the Romans was motivated by their aspiration to secure the Danube – the northern border of the empire.

The members of the legions IV Scythia and V Macedonica, which came to the territory of the Danubian limes at the end of the 2nd century BC, played a crucial role in the establishment of the first legion camps and settlements at the border.

Moesia was transformed into a province in AD 15, but the events on the Danube in the second half of the 1st century (AD 86) resulted in the division of Moesia into Upper and Lower. From then on, until the end of antiquity, the legions VII Claudia and Legion IV Flavia were the permanent guards of Upper Moesia.

The first city-type settlements were built around the already existing autochthonous tribal settlements.

Legion camps and city settlements were built in the area between the confluence of the Morava into the Danube and the Lower Moesia border: Margum (Dubravica), Viminacium (Kostolac), and a larger number of smaller settlements and forts: Lederata (Ram), Pincum (Veliko Gradište), Cuppae (Golubac), Novae (Dobra). Moreover, 18 miles south of Viminacium, Mansio Municipium (Kalište) was located, and even further to the south – Iovis Pagus (Veliko Laole). Legion camps – castra – were located in Viminacium and, briefly, in Margum. The auxiliary departments or parts of the VII Claudia and IV Flavia legions were stationed in the smaller settlements and fortresses (vici, castella).

The primary activity of the legions on the Danube was the construction of communication routes that connected the fortresses along the border. At the same time, these roads represented the connection with Italy, as they were built in the continuation of the Pannonian roads.

At the confluence of Mlava into the Danube, three communications merged, connecting the northern part of the Balkan peninsula with the East, West and South. In addition to military purposes, the roads also enabled dynamic trade.

Viminacium was the largest city and the centre of the province of Upper Moesia, and it remained the permanent camp of the legion VII Claudia until the end of antiquity.

In the network of ancient settlements and the system of urbanization, with the status of a municipium and then of a colony, Viminacium became the main centre of the Roman lifestyle and new social, ethnic, economic and cultural relations.

Urban lifestyle slowly suppressed the tribal one, but the process of adaptation of old tribal centres into new self-governing units, civitates and settlements with municipal rights, was executed carefully, as the autochthonous population wanted to maintain its cultural identity.

At the beginning of the 2nd century, the Braničevo region experienced sudden economic growth. The exploitation of natural resources intensified, influencing the frequency of the trade. The 3rd century crisis of the empire caused by frequent invasions of the barbarians briefly interrupted its economic and cultural prosperity.

In the middle of the 3rd century, the garrisons of legion VII Claudia regained their political influence, and the Danube border – limes, resumed its position as an important strategic point.

In the 5th century, in the year 441AD, the majority of settlements and fortresses were destroyed by the Huns. At the end of the 5th century, this area was settled by the German tribes.

The period of Justinian in the 6th century was marked by the restoration of the majority of the destroyed fortresses. The end of the 6th century marks the end of antiquity and the arrival of the Slavs represented a new phase in the cultural-historical development of this area.

Economic Development

During several centuries of the Empire, the area of the Danubian limes represented a contact zone between the developed countries in the south and the highly civilized provinces in the west.

With the consolidation of the Roman military authority in the second half of the 1st century, served veterans and merchants started permanently settling in the area, enabling the sudden economic growth that culminated in the 2nd and the beginning of the 3rd century. At that time, the entire population was Romanized, and the Roman lifestyle prevailed in all aspects of public and private life.

Economic development and urbanization were more thorough in the large city centres, like Viminacium and Margum, from where the influences were spread to rural areas.

The abundant natural resources and the potential of the wider Viminacium territory, as well as the metal-rich hinterland in the south, attracted veterans, merchants, and other colonists.

With the declaration of Viminacium (AD 117) and Margum (2nd century) as municipal, and later Viminacium as a colony (AD 239), the economy of the region started thriving, with dynamic local production which was influenced by the artisans from northern Italy, Greece and Asia Minor.

The class of city aristocracy was gradually formed, which intensified private and public construction work. The Roman lifestyle and economic prosperity of the population were reflected in the material culture – tools, pottery, tableware, glass objects, jewellery, artisanal products, architectural ornaments and plumbing systems.

The activities of military and civilian artisanal workshops are attested by numerous items necessary for everyday, artisanal or military use.

Viminacium brick workshops had dynamic production. The bricks bear the marks of Legions VII Claudia, and IV Flavia, as well as the marks of private workshops. These products were exported even beyond the territory of Upper Moesia.

With the archaeologically confirmed pottery and brick industry, local mint and indications for other artisanal workshops and art ateliers (stonesmasonry, sculpture, casting, glyptic, glasswork, blacksmith, etc.), Viminacium reflected the dynamic economic changes and transformation in the entire province of Upper Moesia.

Roman Medicine in Viminacium

The arrival of the Roman legions on the territory of Viminacium caused significant socio-political, social and cultural changes that affected all segments of public and private life. The strong concentration of the army, merchants and Roman citizens led to the spread of the cult of Aesculapius and the acclamation of Greek doctors and surgeons, whose work was based on scientific principles.

The development of medicine in Viminacium is recorded in ancient historiography, original medical writings, medical prescriptions and Roman law, which precisely regulated the health service in cities. The law from the time of Antoninus Pius (138–161) allowed the practice of five doctors in small, seven in bigger, and ten in capital cities, like Viminacium.

The indications of advanced medicine and medical services can be found in the archaeological material that enables in-depth research of certain medical fields.

Several graves from Viminacium probably belonged to doctors, but three of them bear special significance. The first was from the 3rd century and in it “medicus chirurgus VII Claudia”, was buried, together with surgical instruments (two probes, two tweezers, two probes, a spatula and a tube-box for storing instruments). The second tomb contained cautery, a probe, tweezers and a trepanation instrument.

The most important was a grave from the last decades of the 1st century and the beginning of the 2nd century. A doctor – specialist in eye diseases – medicus et chirurgus ocularius – was buried with his entire medical kit. The instrumentarium consisted of the instruments placed in the box: four scalpels, two needle hooks for fixing muscles and tissues, tweezers, a cataract needle and a lever. The compartments of the bronze box contained medications of a compact structure in the form of pastilles (pills) and the medications in the form of powder. The stone pallet, was located under the medicine box. These instruments and the chemical composition of the medications (saffron, myrtle and metal oxides) indicate that the person was an eye doctor and surgeon, who performed very complex eye surgeries, curing cataracts and trachoma. These medications found in the box were used to treat inflammatory eye processes, and to sprinkle them on wounds.

Art and culture

The Roman domination in the Braničevo district area, especially during the 2nd and 3rd centuries, was accompanied by the dynamic activity of numerous artisanal workshops and art ateliers, as well as the development of art and culture.

Urbanization and innovations spread from Viminacium, a highly urbanized centre and metropolis, to other smaller settlements along the Danube and in the interior of the province.

Viminacium culture had Roman characteristics, but the influx of settlers - Italics and people from the western and Hellenized areas of the Empire, enriched life and art with certain cosmopolitan elements.

In addition to imported goods of high technical and artistic quality, local artisans and artists produced quality copies based on imported examples, combining them with certain original elements and prominent autochthonous influences. Cultural syncretism, as a synthesis of various techniques, styles, and motifs, also characterized the artwork and artisanal products of Viminacium’s masters.

Decorative items, cosmetic accessories, jewellery and clothing elements, small bronze objects, glyptic art, and works of stonemasons and sculptors, as well as fresco-painted public objects and tombs, attest to the high standards of the private and public life of Viminacium’s residents.

Private and public buildings (administration, religion, trade, entertainment) were lavishly equipped with mosaics, frescoes, decorative architectural plastic, and other interior elements characteristic of the Italic lifestyle.

Frescoes in burial chambers from the 4th century were painted by local artists, mixing themes from Christianity with influences and symbols from pagan religions.

Due to this unique style, especially in portrait painting, certain works of Viminacium’s painting atelier represent the peak of late antique art.

Religion, Cult and Spiritual Life

With the arrival of the Romans, new social relations were created, characteristic of the central parts of the Empire. In religion and cult, Roman culture and autochthonous traditions were combined, resulting in a special spiritual syncretism, especially prominent in the 3rd century.

The influx of foreigners from Hellenized areas and the immigration of veterans led to the acceptance of new cults and deities, gradually surpassing the Roman ones in popularity. As a result of this spiritual syncretism, a specific polytheistic pantheon was created, consisting of Capitoline, Syrian, Greco-Egyptian, Persian, and old Balkan deities.

Votive monuments and votive icons, stone sculptures, and data collected from the excavations of the southern necropolis of Viminacium provided important archaeological insight into religion and cults. These data confirmed the worship of all the most important Capitoline deities: Jupiter, Juno, Minerva, Mercury, Liber and Libera, Bacchus, Asclepius, Ceres, Diana, Hercules, Venus, Apollo, Sol, as well as genii—protectors of the community and individuals, objects, and places.

Deities of eastern origin worshipped in Viminacium were: Cybele (“Great Mother”), Syrian Jupiter Dolichenus, Attis, Serapis, and especially Mithra, a Persian deity who had a temple dedicated to him in Viminacium and was widely accepted by soldiers and merchants. Eastern cults gained more and more popularity in certain social strata over time, and mystical cults gave new explanations about the origin of the world, codes of personal conduct, the afterlife, and resurrection.

Autochthonous traditions and beliefs were still highly represented by the “cult of the Thracian Horseman.” In the 3rd and 4th centuries, the cult of the so-called “Danubian Horsemen” (the lunar goddess) became extremely popular. Numerous votive lead icons with characteristic imagery found in the wider Balkan area attest to the widespread practice of this cult. The discovery of a stone mould indicates that these icons were manufactured in Viminacium.

Christianity, as a new religion, appeared later. Written sources from the 4th century mention Viminacium as an episcopal seat. The new religion brought different funeral and post-funeral rituals, including motifs and themes from Christian mythology depicted in the frescoed tombs from Viminacium.
