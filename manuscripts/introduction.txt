Viminacium: Capital of Upper Moesia

Viminacium was founded in the mid-1st century AD near modern Kostolac, although an earlier tribal stronghold likely existed on the site.

Established by Legio VII Claudia with support from Legio IV Flavia, it grew into the largest city and administrative center of Upper Moesia. Alongside the military camp, a civilian settlement known as a canabae legionaris developed.

Viminacium played an important role in Trajan's Dacian Wars and gained municipal status under Emperor Hadrian in 117 AD. During the Severan dynasty, the city reached its economic peak, becoming a center of trade, craftsmanship, and administration.

In 239 AD, under Gordian III, Viminacium became a Roman colony with the right to mint its own coinage. By the 4th century, it was also an important episcopal seat.

The city was devastated by the Huns in 441 AD, later restored under Justinian, and finally fell to the Avars in 584. The arrival of the Slavs in the 7th century marked the end of ancient Viminacium.

Military Presence on the Danube and the Construction of the Limes

The Roman presence in the region was driven by the need to secure the Danube, the Empire's northern frontier.

Following the division of Moesia in 86 AD, Legio VII Claudia and Legio IV Flavia became the permanent defenders of Upper Moesia. A network of military camps, towns, forts, and roads was established along the Danube, with Viminacium serving as the region's principal military and administrative center.

The legions built roads connecting frontier settlements and linking the Balkans with the wider Roman world. These routes encouraged trade as well as military movement.

Viminacium became the leading center of Roman culture, administration, and urban life in Upper Moesia. Economic prosperity flourished during the 2nd century, though it was disrupted by the crises and invasions of the 3rd century.

The Hunnic invasions of 441 AD destroyed many settlements, while Justinian's restoration efforts briefly revived the region before the arrival of the Slavs marked the end of Antiquity.

Economic Development

The Danubian frontier became a vibrant contact zone connecting the Balkans with the provinces of the Roman Empire.

From the late 1st century onward, veterans, merchants, and settlers transformed the region's economy. Prosperity peaked during the 2nd and early 3rd centuries, when Roman culture dominated both public and private life.

Viminacium and Margum became major urban centers whose influence extended into the countryside. Rich natural resources, strategic trade routes, and municipal privileges encouraged economic growth.

Local workshops produced pottery, bricks, glassware, metal goods, sculptures, and jewelry. Viminacium's brickworks, operated by both military and private workshops, exported products far beyond Upper Moesia.

Together with its mint and numerous artisan workshops, Viminacium became one of the most important economic centers in the province.

Roman Medicine in Viminacium

The arrival of the Roman army brought significant social and cultural changes, including the development of professional medicine.

Roman medical practice, strongly influenced by Greek science and the cult of Aesculapius, was regulated by imperial law. As the provincial capital, Viminacium could support a large number of physicians.

Evidence of advanced medical care comes from several graves belonging to doctors buried with their instruments. Surgical tools such as probes, tweezers, scalpels, hooks, and cautery instruments reveal a high level of medical expertise.

The most remarkable discovery is the grave of an eye surgeon from the late 1st or early 2nd century. Buried with a complete medical kit, medicines, and specialized surgical instruments, he treated conditions such as cataracts and eye infections.

These finds demonstrate that Viminacium possessed a sophisticated medical tradition comparable to that of other major Roman cities.
